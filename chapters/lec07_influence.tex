\section{Lecture 7: Path integrals: the influence functional, the polaron frame and the noninteracting-blip approximation}
\label{sec:lec7}

So far we have organized the perturbation series by what the bath does to the \emph{system superoperators}.
The path integral does the opposite: it replaces the system operators by c-number paths, so that the bath can be integrated out \emph{exactly} into an exponential, the Feynman--Vernon influence functional~\cite{FeynmanVernon1963}.
For a qubit the paths are discrete, which leads to the blip--sojourn expansion of Leggett \emph{et al.}~\cite{Leggett1987} and to the noninteracting-blip approximation (NIBA), the workhorse of the dissipative two-level system.
We show that NIBA is nothing but the second-order Nakajima--Zwanzig kernel in the polaron frame.

\subsection{The influence functional as Wick's theorem with a commutative system}
\label{sec:FV-derivation}

Suppose the system superoperators at different times commuted.
Then the sum over all pairings of $2n$ vertices would be $\frac{1}{n!}$ times the $n$-th power of the single contraction, and the whole Dyson series \eqref{eq:dyson-super} would exponentiate.
Operators do not commute, but in a path integral they are replaced by c-number paths: on the forward branch $S\to s^+(t)$, on the backward branch $S\to s^-(t)$, and in rotated variables $s^\cl=\frac12(s^++s^-)$, $s^\qu=s^+-s^-$.
Repeating \cref{sec:feynman-rules} with c-number vertices, the sum over pairings exponentiates to
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \Phi[s^\cl,s^\qu]=-\int_0^t\!\dd t_1\!\int_0^{t_1}\!\dd t_2\,
  \Big[s^\qu(t_1)\,S(t_1-t_2)\,s^\qu(t_2)-i\,s^\qu(t_1)\,\chi(t_1-t_2)\,s^\cl(t_2)\Big],\;}
  \label{eq:FV}
\end{equation}
and the reduced density matrix is
\begin{equation}
  \rhoS(t)=\int\!\mathcal Ds^+\mathcal Ds^-\;\ee^{\,iA_S[s^+]-iA_S[s^-]+\Phi[s^\cl,s^\qu]}\;(\text{boundary terms}),
\end{equation}
with $A_S$ the system action.
In the original $\pm$ variables and with $C=S-\tfrac i2\chi$, \cref{eq:FV} is Feynman and Vernon's $\Phi=-\int_{t_1>t_2}\big(s^+_1-s^-_1\big)\big[C(t_1-t_2)s^+_2-C^*(t_1-t_2)s^-_2\big]$, which the reader should check (Exercise~\ref{ex:7-1}).
The rotated form \cref{eq:FV} says: the \emph{noise} kernel couples the quantum path to itself, the \emph{response} kernel couples the quantum path to the earlier classical path, and there is no $\cl$--$\cl$ term.
Equivalently, $\Phi=-\frac12\int\!\!\int s^aD^{ab}s^b$ with $D$ the Larkin--Ovchinnikov matrix \eqref{eq:LO}: this is how the influence functional appears when a Gaussian bath is integrated out of the Keldysh action~\cite[Sec.~3.2]{Kamenev2011}, \cite[Secs.~3--4]{Grabert1988}, \cite[Ch.~5]{Weiss2012}.
For a single Gaussian bath, \cref{eq:FV} contains \emph{all} the information that any method uses; they differ only in how they handle the non-commutativity that the path integral pushes into the measure.

\begin{exbox}[Example 7.1: reading the influence functional]
\emph{(i) Pure dephasing.}
With $S=\sz$ and the system restricted to the coherence $\ket0\bra1$, the path variables are frozen at $s^+=+1$, $s^-=-1$, so $s^\cl=0$ and $s^\qu=2$.
Then $\Phi=-4\int_{t_1>t_2}S(t_1-t_2)=-\Gamma(t)$ and $\rho_{01}(t)=\rho_{01}(0)\ee^{-i\omega_0t-\Gamma(t)}$, reproducing \cref{eq:dephasing-exact} in one line.
The coherence is a path on which the two branches maximally disagree, and only the noise sees it.

\emph{(ii) Semiclassical limit and Langevin equation.}
$\Phi$ is at most quadratic in $s^\qu$.
Decouple the quadratic term with an auxiliary real field $\xi$ (Hubbard--Stratonovich), $\exp[-\tfrac12\!\int\!\!\int s^\qu Ss^\qu]=\int\!\mathcal D\xi\, \exp[-\tfrac12\!\int\!\!\int\xi S^{-1}\xi+i\!\int\!\xi s^\qu]$, and expand the system action to first order in $s^\qu$ (exact for a harmonic system).
The $s^\qu$ integral then produces a delta functional enforcing an equation of motion for $s^\cl$ with damping kernel $\chi$ and a Gaussian force $\xi$ of correlator $S$: the quantum Langevin equation~\cite{FordKacMazur1965,CaldeiraLeggett1983}.
The $\qu$ field has become the Martin--Siggia--Rose response field~\cite{MartinSiggiaRose1973}, the $\qu\qu=0$ rule of \cref{eq:LO} is the statement that the response field has no self-correlation (causality), and in the high-temperature limit $S(\omega)\to2T\chi''(\omega)/\omega$ is the classical FDT~\cite[Ch.~4]{Kamenev2011}.
\Cref{sec:lec8} returns to this from Mori's side.
\end{exbox}

\subsection{Spin paths: blips and sojourns}

For a qubit there is no continuous coordinate, but \cref{eq:FV} still applies if we expand in the part of $H_S$ that does not commute with $S$.
Use the Leggett form \eqref{eq:leggett-form}: $S=\sz'$ is diagonal, the bias $\varepsilon$ is diagonal, and the tunnelling $-\tfrac\Delta2\sx'$ flips the spin.
Expanding $\ee^{-iHt}$ and $\ee^{iHt}$ in $\Delta$, each branch becomes a piecewise-constant path $s^\pm(t)=\pm1$ with flips at the vertices (a factor $i\Delta/2$ each, and the bias phase $\ee^{\mp i\varepsilon s^\pm\dd t/2}$ along the way).
The pair $(s^+,s^-)$ then alternates between \emph{sojourns}, $s^+=s^-$ (so $s^\qu=0$, $s^\cl=\pm1$), and \emph{blips}, $s^+=-s^-$ (so $s^\qu=\pm2$, $s^\cl=0$)~\cite[Sec.~IV]{Leggett1987}, \cite[Ch.~21]{Weiss2012}.
In Keldysh language a sojourn is a segment where the two branches agree, a classical configuration, and a blip is where they differ, a quantum excursion.
Inserting such paths in \cref{eq:FV}, two integrations by parts convert the kernels into the functions
\begin{equation}
  Q_2(\tau)=\frac4\pi\int_0^\infty\!\dd\omega\,\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega^2}\coth\tfrac{\beta\omega}2\,(1-\cos\omega\tau),\qquad
  Q_1(\tau)=\frac4\pi\int_0^\infty\!\dd\omega\,\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega^2}\sin\omega\tau,
  \label{eq:Q12}
\end{equation}
with $\ddot Q_2=4S$ and $\ddot Q_1=-2\chi$ for $\tau>0$ (the factor 4 is again ``$\sz$ versus $\sz/2$''; with Leggett's coupling the prefactor is $1/\pi$).
The influence functional becomes a sum of pairwise interactions: blip--blip through $Q_2$ (noise), and blip--sojourn through $Q_1$ (response), with the sojourn \emph{preceding} the blip, as causality requires.

\begin{keybox}[The noninteracting-blip approximation]
Keep, for each blip of duration $\tau$, only its self-interaction $\ee^{-Q_2(\tau)}$ and its interaction with the immediately preceding sojourn, $\ee^{\mp iQ_1(\tau)}$; drop all interactions between different blips and between a blip and earlier sojourns.
The resulting series for the population $P(t)\equiv\avg{\sz'}_t$ sums to the integro-differential equation
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\dot P(t)=-\int_0^t\!\dd\tau\Big[K^s(\tau)\,P(t-\tau)+K^a(\tau)\Big],\qquad
  \begin{aligned}
  K^s(\tau)&=\Delta^2\,\ee^{-Q_2(\tau)}\cos Q_1(\tau)\cos\varepsilon\tau,\\
  K^a(\tau)&=\Delta^2\,\ee^{-Q_2(\tau)}\sin Q_1(\tau)\sin\varepsilon\tau .
  \end{aligned}\;}
  \label{eq:NIBA}
\end{equation}
\end{keybox}
This is the NIBA of Leggett \emph{et al.}~\cite[Sec.~V]{Leggett1987}; see \cite[Ch.~21]{Weiss2012} for its many extensions.
It is exact to leading order in $\Delta$; it is also exact for the Ohmic bath at $\alpha=\tfrac12$ (Toulouse) and very accurate at high temperature or strong damping, where blips are short and dilute.
It fails for a biased system at low temperature and weak damping, where the long-time Boltzmann weights come out wrong (Exercise~\ref{ex:7-4}); the reason will be transparent below.

\subsection{NIBA is the second-order NZ kernel in the polaron frame}

The blip language suggests a unitary transformation: a blip is a segment in which the bath is displaced in opposite directions on the two branches.
Displace it once and for all with the polaron transformation~\cite{SilbeyHarris1984}
\begin{equation}
  U=\exp\Big[-\sz'\sum_k\frac{g_k}{\omega_k}\big(b_k^\dagger-b_k\big)\Big],\qquad
  U^\dagger b_kU=b_k-\sz'\frac{g_k}{\omega_k},
\end{equation}
which gives $U^\dagger(H_B+\sz'B)U=H_B-\sum_kg_k^2/\omega_k$: the diagonal coupling is removed at the price of dressing the tunnelling,
\begin{equation}
  H'=U^\dagger HU=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sz'+H_B-\frac{\Delta}{2}\big(\sigma_+'B_++\sigma_-'B_-\big)+\text{const},\qquad
  B_\pm=\exp\Big[\pm2\sum_k\frac{g_k}{\omega_k}\big(b_k^\dagger-b_k\big)\Big].
  \label{eq:polaron-H}
\end{equation}
Now treat $V=-\tfrac\Delta2(\sigma_+'B_++\sigma_-'B_-)$ as the perturbation and use the NZ equation with Zwanzig's \emph{diagonal} projector $\Pc_dX=\sum_{i=\uparrow\downarrow}\ket i\bra i\otimes\rhoB\,\Tr_B\bra iX\ket i$, which keeps only the populations.
Since $V$ is off-diagonal, $\Pc_dV\Pc_d=0$ exactly and the second-order kernel needs no $\Qc$:
\begin{equation}
  \dot P_\uparrow(t)=\int_0^t\!\dd\tau\Big[w_{\uparrow\leftarrow\downarrow}(\tau)P_\downarrow(t-\tau)-w_{\downarrow\leftarrow\uparrow}(\tau)P_\uparrow(t-\tau)\Big],
  \qquad w_{\uparrow\leftarrow\downarrow}(\tau)=\frac{\Delta^2}{2}\Re\Big[\ee^{i\varepsilon\tau}\avg{B_-(0)B_+(\tau)}\Big],
\end{equation}
and $w_{\downarrow\leftarrow\uparrow}(\tau)=\frac{\Delta^2}2\Re[\ee^{-i\varepsilon\tau}\avg{B_+(0)B_-(\tau)}]$.
The dressing operators are displacement operators, and the thermal expectation of a product of two displacements is a Gaussian (Exercise~\ref{ex:7-3}):
\begin{equation}
  \avg{B_-(0)B_+(\tau)}=\avg{B_+(0)B_-(\tau)}=\ee^{-Q_2(\tau)+iQ_1(\tau)},
  \label{eq:FC}
\end{equation}
with exactly the $Q_{1,2}$ of \cref{eq:Q12}.
Hence $w_{\downarrow\leftarrow\uparrow}=\tfrac{\Delta^2}2\ee^{-Q_2}\cos(\varepsilon\tau-Q_1)$, $w_{\uparrow\leftarrow\downarrow}=\tfrac{\Delta^2}2\ee^{-Q_2}\cos(\varepsilon\tau+Q_1)$, and with $P=P_\uparrow-P_\downarrow$, $P_{\uparrow\downarrow}=\tfrac12(1\pm P)$, the two rate equations combine into \cref{eq:NIBA} with $K^s=w_{\downarrow\leftarrow\uparrow}+w_{\uparrow\leftarrow\downarrow}$ and $K^a=w_{\downarrow\leftarrow\uparrow}-w_{\uparrow\leftarrow\downarrow}$.
This identification is due to Aslangul, Pottier and Saint-James~\cite{Aslangul1986} and Dekker~\cite{Dekker1987}.

\begin{keybox}[Key point 7.1]
NIBA $=$ Born approximation (second-order NZ) for the populations, in the polaron frame, with the full (unsubtracted) Franck--Condon correlator \eqref{eq:FC}.
Interblip interactions are the fourth- and higher-order irreducible diagrams of Theorem~\ref{thm:irreducible} in that frame.
The two expansions of the course, in $g$ (\cref{sec:lec5}) and in $\Delta$ (this lecture), are second-order NZ kernels with respect to two different projectors and two different ``free'' dynamics.
\end{keybox}

\begin{exbox}[Example 7.2: where NIBA goes wrong, and why]
The steady state of \cref{eq:NIBA} is $P_\infty=-\hat K^a(0)/\hat K^s(0)$.
At weak coupling expand $\ee^{-Q_2}\cos Q_1\approx1-Q_2$, $\ee^{-Q_2}\sin Q_1\approx Q_1$; the $\tau$-integrals pick out $J(\varepsilon)$ and give
\begin{equation}
  \hat K^s(0)\simeq\frac{2\Delta^2}{\varepsilon^2}J(\varepsilon)\coth\frac{\beta\varepsilon}{2},\qquad
  \hat K^a(0)\simeq\frac{2\Delta^2}{\varepsilon^2}J(\varepsilon),\qquad P_\infty\simeq-\tanh\frac{\beta\varepsilon}{2}.
\end{equation}
The individual rates are $w_{\downarrow\leftarrow\uparrow}\simeq2\Delta^2J(\varepsilon)[1+n(\varepsilon)]/\varepsilon^2$ and $w_{\uparrow\leftarrow\downarrow}\simeq2\Delta^2J(\varepsilon)n(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon^2$: golden-rule rates with detailed balance, but with the bath sampled at the \emph{bias} $\varepsilon$ and weighted by $(\Delta/\varepsilon)^2$.
Compare \cref{eq:rates-T1}: the correct weak-coupling rates sample the bath at the \emph{level splitting} $\omega_0=\sqrt{\varepsilon^2+\Delta^2}$ with weight $\sin^2\theta=(\Delta/\omega_0)^2$, and the correct $P_\infty$ is the Gibbs state of $H_S$, $-\cos\theta\tanh(\beta\omega_0/2)$ along $\sz'$.
The two agree only for $\Delta\ll\varepsilon$.
The second-order-in-$\Delta$ kernel cannot know that the true eigenstates are superpositions of the pointer states; that information sits in the interblip diagrams.
Conversely, the expansion in $g$ cannot describe the strong-coupling regime where $Q_2$ is large and tunnelling is incoherent, which NIBA handles well.
Each projector has its domain.
\end{exbox}

\subsection{From the influence functional to the memory kernel: the construction of Ivander, Lindoy and Lee}
\label{sec:ill}

Theorem~\ref{thm:irreducible} expresses the NZ kernel as the sum of irreducible diagrams, with the bath lines written out one by one.
The influence functional \eqref{eq:FV} keeps the same lines exponentiated.
It is natural to ask for the kernel directly in terms of $\ee^{\Phi}$, and the question has a practical side: numerically exact path-integral methods (the quasi-adiabatic propagator path integral of Makri and Makarov~\cite{MakriMakarov1995} and its descendants, the small-matrix path integral~\cite{Makri2020}, tensor-network contractions of the influence functional) all work with a time-discretized $\ee^\Phi$, and a generalized master equation needs $K$.
Ivander, Lindoy and Lee~\cite{IvanderLindoyLee2024} answered it for a discretized path integral: they wrote the discrete-time kernel $K_n$ as an explicit polynomial in the ``influence functions'' $I_k$ into which $\ee^\Phi$ factorizes, organized the terms by Dyck paths, and inverted the relation, so that the $I_k$, and from them the bath correlation function and $J(\omega)$, can be read off from the reduced dynamics.
Their ``Class 1'', a single coupling operator $S$ per bath, is exactly our model in the $S$ eigenbasis.
This subsection presents the construction in our notation and proves it from Lemma~\ref{lem:incl-excl}, which makes it the discrete-time version of Theorem~\ref{thm:irreducible}; a numerical check and a Hamiltonian-learning example on the Lorentzian bath follow.

\subsubsection{The discretized path integral}

Work in the eigenbasis of $S$ (the Leggett form \eqref{eq:leggett-form}, $S=\sz'$), with the Liouville index $x=(s^+,s^-)\in\{\pm1\}^2$ labelling the four elements $\ket{s^+}\bra{s^-}$.
Split each step of length $\Delta t$ symmetrically, $\ee^{-iH\Delta t}\simeq\ee^{-iH_S\Delta t/2}\ee^{-i(H_B+S B)\Delta t}\ee^{-iH_S\Delta t/2}$, with error $O(\Delta t^3)$ per step.
The middle factor is diagonal in the $S$ eigenbasis, so on a path the system variables are piecewise constant, $s^\pm(t)=s^\pm_k$ on the $k$-th cell, and the bath is a set of oscillators driven by the c-number force $s_k$.
Tracing it out gives \cref{eq:FV} evaluated on piecewise-constant paths, which is a double sum over cells:
\begin{equation}
  \ee^{\Phi}=\prod_{k=1}^{N}I_0(x_k)\prod_{k>k'}I_{k-k'}(x_k,x_{k'}),\qquad
  I_n(x',x)=\exp\!\big[-(s'^+-s'^-)\big(\eta_n\,s^+-\eta_n^*\,s^-\big)\big],
  \label{eq:ill-I}
\end{equation}
where $x'$ is the later cell, $\eta_n=\int_{\text{cell }k}\!\dd t\int_{\text{cell }k-n}\!\dd t'\,C(t-t')$ for $n\ge1$ and $\eta_0=\int_{\text{cell}}\dd t\int_{\text{cell},\,t'<t}\dd t'\,C(t-t')$ for the self-interaction of one cell; stationarity of the bath makes $\eta$ depend on the cell separation only.
For the Lorentzian bath at $T=0$, $C(t)=g^2\ee^{-at}$ with $a=i\Omega+\gamma/2$, the integrals are elementary,
\begin{equation}
  \eta_n=\frac{4g^2\sinh^2(a\Delta t/2)}{a^2}\,\ee^{-an\Delta t}\ (n\ge1),\qquad
  \eta_0=\frac{g^2}{a}\Big[\Delta t-\frac{1-\ee^{-a\Delta t}}{a}\Big].
  \label{eq:ill-eta}
\end{equation}
The system enters through the half-step propagator $G=\ee^{\Lc_S\Delta t/2}$, a $4\times4$ matrix in the $x$ basis, and $F=G^2$; the reduced propagator after $N$ steps is the contraction
\begin{equation}
  U_N=G\,\Big[\sum_{x_1\dots x_N}\prod_{k}I_0(x_k)\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}F_{x_{k+1}x_k}\prod_{k>k'}I_{k-k'}(x_k,x_{k'})\Big]\,G,
  \label{eq:ill-U}
\end{equation}
with the outer $G$'s attached to the initial and final indices.
Because the half steps sit at the ends, every object in \cref{eq:ill-U} is translation invariant along the path; this is the point of the symmetric ordering, and it is the convention that makes the kernel below a function of a single time difference.
With $4^N$ paths the sum is a toy for $N\lesssim8$, which is all we need.
For $g=0.3$, $\theta=\pi/4$ the script finds $|U_N-\Pi(N\Delta t)|=8\times10^{-5},\ 1\times10^{-5},\ 1.3\times10^{-6}$ at $N=1$ for $\Delta t=0.2,0.1,0.05$, the expected $O(\Delta t^3)$ per step, with $\Pi$ the exact pseudomode map rotated to the $S$ basis.

\subsubsection{The discrete NZ equation and the transfer tensors}

Define the discrete kernels $K_n$ by the discrete-time Nakajima--Zwanzig equation
\begin{equation}
  \rhoS(t_N)=F\,\rhoS(t_{N-1})+\Delta t^2\sum_{m=1}^{N}K_{N-m}\,\rhoS(t_{m-1}),\qquad
  U_N=F\,U_{N-1}+\Delta t^2\sum_{m=1}^{N}K_{N-m}\,U_{m-1},
  \label{eq:ill-NZ}
\end{equation}
which is the rectangle-rule discretization of \cref{eq:NZ} with $F=\ee^{\Lc_S\Delta t}$ as the free step (Ivander \emph{et al.} use $1-i\Lc_S\Delta t$, the same to the order kept).
Solving \cref{eq:ill-NZ} upward in $N$ gives
\begin{equation}
  K_0=\frac{U_1-F}{\Delta t^2},\qquad
  K_1=\frac{U_2-U_1U_1}{\Delta t^2},\qquad
  K_2=\frac{U_3-U_2U_1-U_1U_2+U_1U_1U_1}{\Delta t^2},\qquad\dots
  \label{eq:ill-ttm}
\end{equation}
These are the transfer tensors of Cerrillo and Cao~\cite{CerrilloCao2014}, and the pattern is Lemma~\ref{lem:incl-excl} once more: $\Delta t^2K_n$ is the alternating sum, over all subsets $\mathcal C$ of the $n$ interior time points, of the product of propagators cut at $\mathcal C$, $\Delta t^2K_n=\sum_{\mathcal C}(-1)^{|\mathcal C|}U_{\ell_1}U_{\ell_2}\cdots$ with $\ell_i$ the lengths of the segments into which $\mathcal C$ divides $[0,n+1]$.
It is the discrete form of $\hat K(z)=z-\Lc_S-\hat\Pi(z)^{-1}$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:5-4}), and like it, it extracts a kernel from \emph{any} propagator; fed with the exact $\Pi(n\Delta t)$ instead of $U_n$, it returns $K_n=K(n\Delta t)+O(\Delta t)$ (the script finds errors $1.5\times10^{-2},\,7.5\times10^{-3},\,3.8\times10^{-3}$ at $\tau=1$ for $\Delta t=0.2,0.1,0.05$, against $|K|\approx0.07$).

\subsubsection{The kernel in terms of the influence functions}

Insert \cref{eq:ill-U} into \cref{eq:ill-ttm}.
A product $U_aU_b$ is the same path sum as $U_{a+b}$, with $GG=F$ at the junction and the $I_0$'s unchanged, except that every $I_{k-k'}$ whose two cells lie on opposite sides of the junction is replaced by $1$.
Hence cutting at a set $\mathcal C$ of interior points means: set to $1$ every ``arc'' $(k',k)$ that spans a cut.
Now expand each arc factor as $I=1+\tilde I$, $\tilde I\equiv I-1$, so that the full product becomes a sum over subsets $A$ of arcs of $\prod_{(k',k)\in A}\tilde I_{k-k'}(x_k,x_{k'})$.
A given $A$ survives the cut at $\mathcal C$ iff no arc of $A$ spans a point of $\mathcal C$, so the alternating sum over $\mathcal C$ is, by Lemma~\ref{lem:incl-excl}, the sum over those $A$ for which \emph{no} cut is possible, i.e.\ every one of the $n$ gaps is spanned by at least one arc of $A$:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;K_n=\frac1{\Delta t^2}\,G\Big[\sum_{x_0\dots x_n}\prod_{k=0}^{n}I_0(x_k)\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}F_{x_{k+1}x_k}\!\!\sum_{A\ \text{no cut}}\ \prod_{(k',k)\in A}\tilde I_{k-k'}(x_k,x_{k'})\Big]G\;}
  \label{eq:ill-K}
\end{equation}
for $n\ge1$, and $K_0=G\,\mathrm{diag}(\tilde I_0)\,G/\Delta t^2$.
This is Theorem~\ref{thm:irreducible} on the lattice: with $\ell$ the sum of the lines joining cells $k'$ and $k$, $\tilde I_{k-k'}=\ee^{-\ell}-1$ is ``at least one line on this arc'', and ``no cut'' is irreducibility.
The number of no-cut arc sets grows fast ($5,\,49,\,893,\,30\,649$ for $n=2,3,4,5$), but they organize into far fewer terms.
For a no-cut set $A$ let $\mathcal C(A)$ be its maximal arcs, those not contained in another arc of $A$.
Ordered by their left ends they form a \emph{chain}: left and right ends both strictly increasing, the first starting at $0$, the last ending at $n$, and consecutive arcs overlapping or abutting (otherwise a gap would be uncovered).
Every other arc of $A$ lies inside some arc of $\mathcal C(A)$.
Summing over all $A$ with the same chain $\mathcal C$ therefore gives $\prod_{c\in\mathcal C}\tilde I_c$ times, for every arc nested inside a chain arc, the free factor $(1+\tilde I)=I$, and nothing for the remaining arcs:
\begin{equation}
  K_n=\frac1{\Delta t^2}\,G\Big[\sum_{x}\prod I_0\prod F\sum_{\mathcal C\ \text{chain}}\ \prod_{c\in\mathcal C}\tilde I_c\prod_{b\subset\text{some }c\in\mathcal C,\ b\notin\mathcal C}I_b\Big]G .
  \label{eq:ill-chain}
\end{equation}
The chains of $n$ gaps are counted by the Catalan numbers, $1,2,5,14,42,132,\dots$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:7-5}), and they are in bijection with the Dyck paths of semilength $n$ by which Ivander \emph{et al.} enumerate the terms; the Catalan count is their observation that $K_n$ has $C_n$ terms.
Written out,
\begin{align}
  \Delta t^2K_1&=G\,\big[I_0F\tilde I_1I_0\big]\,G,\notag\\
  \Delta t^2K_2&=G\,\big[I_0FI_0FI_0\,\big(\tilde I_{2}^{(02)}I_1^{(01)}I_1^{(12)}+\tilde I_1^{(01)}\tilde I_1^{(12)}\big)\big]\,G,
  \label{eq:ill-K12}\\
  \Delta t^2K_3&=G\,\big[I_0FI_0FI_0FI_0\,\big(\tilde I_3^{(03)}I_2^{(02)}I_2^{(13)}I_1^{(01)}I_1^{(12)}I_1^{(23)}
  +\tilde I_2^{(02)}\tilde I_2^{(13)}I_1^{(01)}I_1^{(12)}I_1^{(23)}\notag\\
  &\qquad+\tilde I_1^{(01)}\tilde I_2^{(13)}I_1^{(12)}I_1^{(23)}+\tilde I_2^{(02)}\tilde I_1^{(23)}I_1^{(01)}I_1^{(12)}+\tilde I_1^{(01)}\tilde I_1^{(12)}\tilde I_1^{(23)}\big)\big]\,G,\notag
\end{align}
where the superscript names the arc and all factors are evaluated on the summed path indices.
The first term of each $K_n$, the single arc $(0,n)$ dressed with every shorter $I$, is what Ivander \emph{et al.} call the crest term; it carries the longest-range line, and it dominates when $\tilde I_k$ decays fast with $k$ (their Ohmic examples), while the last term, $\tilde I_1^{\,n}$, is the only one that survives if the influence functions are truncated at one step, which is how the quasi-adiabatic path integral with memory length one is embedded in the exact kernel.
The script evaluates \cref{eq:ill-chain} for $n\le3$ and compares with \cref{eq:ill-ttm}; they agree to $6\times10^{-14}$, which is the identity \eqref{eq:ill-K} checked with non-commuting $G$'s in place.

\subsubsection{The inverse map, and a degeneracy}

Equation \eqref{eq:ill-chain} is triangular: $K_n$ contains $I_n$ only through the crest term, and there only as the single factor $\tilde I_n(x_n,x_0)$ multiplying a dressing $D_n(x_n,x_0)$ built from $I_0,\dots,I_{n-1}$ and the $F$'s.
So, recursively,
\begin{equation}
  I_0(x)=\big[G^{-1}(\Delta t^2K_0+F)G^{-1}\big]_{xx},\qquad
  \tilde I_n(x',x)=\frac{\big[G^{-1}\Delta t^2K_nG^{-1}\big]_{x'x}-\big[\text{non-crest terms}\big]_{x'x}}{D_n(x',x)},
  \label{eq:ill-inverse}
\end{equation}
with elementwise division, e.g.\ $\tilde I_1(x',x)=[G^{-1}\Delta t^2K_1G^{-1}]_{x'x}/[I_0(x')F_{x'x}I_0(x)]$.
This is the map $\rho\to U\to K\to I$ of Ivander \emph{et al.}; it is exact for data generated by the same discretization, and the script recovers $I_0,\dots,I_3$ from the transfer tensors of the Trotterized $U_N$ to $5\times10^{-15}$.
The division fails where $D_n(x',x)=0$.
For pure dephasing, $H_S$ is diagonal in the $S$ basis, $G$ and $F$ are diagonal, the path indices are frozen, and $D_n$ is diagonal in $(x',x)$: only $\tilde I_n(x,x)$ can be recovered, which for $x=(+,-)$ is $\ee^{-4\Re\eta_n}-1$, the noise part.
The response part $\Im\eta_n$ sits in the off-diagonal elements and is invisible, in agreement with \cref{sec:lec10}: a longitudinal sensor measures $S(\omega)$ and nothing of $\chi$.
Ivander \emph{et al.} note the same degeneracy (``the map is no longer bijective'') and that $J(\omega)$ is still recoverable from the diagonal elements by an inverse cosine transform, which is the statement that $S(\omega)$ determines $J$ at known temperature.

\begin{exbox}[Example 7.3: learning the Lorentzian bath from the reduced dynamics]
Suppose we are given the reduced propagators $U_n$ of the qubit (process tomography at $n\Delta t$) and $H_S$, but not the bath.
The chain $U\to K\to I\to\eta$ gives $\eta_1,\eta_2,\dots$, and \cref{eq:ill-eta} shows that two of them fix the Lorentzian: $a=i\Omega+\gamma/2=-\ln(\eta_2/\eta_1)/\Delta t$, then $g^2$ from $|\eta_1|$.
With Trotter-consistent data ($U_n$ from \cref{eq:ill-U}, $\Delta t=0.1$) the script returns $\Omega=1.000000$, $\gamma=0.500000$, $g^2=0.090000$: the map is an identity.
With the \emph{exact} $\Pi(n\Delta t)$ as input the picture changes: the recovered $\eta_n$ carry the Trotter mismatch, a relative error $1.3\times10^{-2},\,6.3\times10^{-3},\,3.9\times10^{-3},\,2.6\times10^{-3}$ for $n=1,\dots,4$, almost entirely in the phase (the response part, which the symmetric split places half a cell off), and the exponent extracted from consecutive cells amplifies it by $1/\Delta t$: $\gamma$ comes out right ($0.5024$) but $\Omega=0.930$.
Using a longer baseline, $a=-\ln(\eta_{1+m}/\eta_1)/(m\Delta t)$, the bias falls as $1/m$: $\Omega=0.930,\,0.953,\,0.964$ for $m=1,2,3$.
The lesson is the one Ivander \emph{et al.} draw for their Ohmic and sub-Ohmic examples: the inversion is exact, but the precision of a learned $J(\omega)$ is set by the highest kernel order one can afford, and the cost of $K_n$ grows as $4^{n}$ (in general $d^{2n}$) without further approximation.
\end{exbox}

\subsubsection{Assessment}

What the construction adds to Lectures 5 and 7 is the explicit algebra: Theorem~\ref{thm:irreducible} and the transfer-tensor formula both say ``the kernel is the no-cut part of the propagator'', but \cref{eq:ill-chain} says it in the variables a path-integral code actually stores, and it is invertible.
Three qualifications are in order.
First, the kernel is tied to the discretization: a different Trotter ordering changes $K_n$ at $O(\Delta t)$, and the authors' choice is the one that keeps $K_n$ a function of $n$ alone (for a driven system they factor the two-time kernel into a time-dependent system tensor and a time-independent influence tensor).
That the inversion is exact only for data of the same discretization is the practical limitation seen in Example~7.3.
Second, priority: the relation between transfer tensors and influence functions is close to the small-matrix path integral of Makri~\cite{Makri2020}, and a Comment by Makri \emph{et al.}~\cite{MakriComment2024} with a Reply~\cite{IvanderReply2024} dispute whether the kernel--influence-function relation, the endpoint conventions and the Dyck-path bookkeeping were new; the reader can form an opinion from \cref{eq:ill-K}, which is the content of both papers in one line, and from Lemma~\ref{lem:incl-excl}, which is the proof.
Third, scope: \cref{eq:ill-I} needs a Gaussian bath and a single coupling operator per bath (their Class 1); for several non-commuting couplings or a shared bath (Classes 2 and 3) the influence functional no longer factorizes over pairs of cells in one basis, and the authors give generalizations, while for non-diagonalizable couplings (Class 4, the Anderson impurity) there is no basis in which the coupling is a c-number along the path, and the authors use a different bookkeeping that we do not follow here.
Everything in this subsection is for the single coupling $S$ of \cref{eq:model-intro}, which is Class 1 for any $\theta$.

\begin{keybox}[Key point 7.2]
On a time lattice the NZ kernel is the no-cut part of the discretized influence functional: $K_n$ is the sum over sets of arcs covering all $n$ gaps of products of $\tilde I=I-1$, dressed with the free propagators (\cref{eq:ill-K}), which regroups into the Catalan-many chain (Dyck-path) terms of \cref{eq:ill-chain}.
The relation is triangular, hence invertible: the influence functions, and with them $C(t)$ on the grid, follow from the reduced dynamics.
It is exact for the discretization it is derived in, blind to the response part under pure dephasing, and limited in practice by the exponential cost of high kernel orders.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{What the Keldysh field theory adds}

Reading \cref{eq:NIBA} from the Keldysh side: $K^s$ is built from the real part of the dressed propagator $\ee^{-Q}$ (the Keldysh component), $K^a$ from the imaginary part (the retarded component).
The full blip--sojourn series is a Keldysh diagrammatics in which blips are quantum lines and sojourns are classical ones, and a renormalization-group (RG) treatment of it is the real-time RG of Keil and Schoeller~\cite{Keil2001}.
We return to the field-theory formulation of the qubit in \cref{sec:lec9}.
